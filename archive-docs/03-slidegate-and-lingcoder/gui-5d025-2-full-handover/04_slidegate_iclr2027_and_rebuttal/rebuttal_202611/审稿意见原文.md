## 审稿人 6R1G

Official Review of Submission39487 by Reviewer 6R1G — 22 Oct 2026, 17:56 (modified: 06 Nov 2026, 20:31)

**Paper Summary:**
This paper introduces SlideGate, a benchmark for evaluating AI-generated presentations across instruction following, content quality, and visual presentation. The benchmark contains 40 tasks covering seven application scenarios and five input types, with 2191 task-specific verification items. The proposed evaluation framework combines automatically constructed verification criteria, rule-based checks, source-grounded content assessment, and visual inspection to identify problems such as missing required content, instruction violations, and layout defects. The authors evaluate 240 presentations generated by six commercial systems and find that existing overall quality scores often fail to reliably identify serious presentation errors. They further validate the evaluation framework through human annotations, designer preferences, and comparisons with existing evaluation methods.

**Presentation:** 3: Clear and easy to follow.
**Informativeness:** 2: I learned something interesting or useful.
**Soundness:** 2: I have minor or fixable concerns about the paper's soundness.

**Critical Strengths:**
Evaluating whether generated slides actually meet users' requirements is challenging, as a presentation can look visually appealing while still missing key information or violating explicit instructions. The benchmark covers a range of real-world scenarios and input types, making it potentially useful for evaluating presentation-generation systems in practical settings.

**Critical Weaknesses:**
- The main observation that overall quality scores may hide specific errors is reasonable but relatively straightforward. The proposed framework also combines several established evaluation techniques, including instruction verification, content coverage checks, and visual defect detection. Similar aspects have already been studied in existing benchmarks such as PresentBench, PPTBench, and SlideAudit. I think the paper should more clearly demonstrate what new evaluation capabilities SlideGate provides beyond these existing approaches.
- Although the paper evaluates six commercial systems, it does not include several widely used alternatives, such as ChatGPT, Claude, NotebookLM, or Microsoft Copilot for PowerPoint. These systems support different presentation-generation workflows, particularly document-based generation and slide editing, which are relevant to the benchmark's evaluation dimensions. Including a broader and more diverse range of systems would make the benchmark more representative and provide stronger evidence that the findings generalize beyond the six selected systems.

**Paper Summary:**
In this paper, the authors propose a presentation generation benchmark, named SlideGate, which contains 40 presentation-generation tasks and can effectively evaluate LLMs on instruction following, content quality and visual presentation. Experimental results show that the benchmark can diagnose failures of different types.

**Presentation:** 2: Generally understandable, but requires substantial effort in places.
**Informativeness:** 2: I learned something interesting or useful.
**Soundness:** 2: I have minor or fixable concerns about the paper's soundness.

**Critical Strengths:**
Presentation generation is a widely-used task skill, therefore its evaluation is helpful. Compared with previous quality score-based evaluation, the benchmark proposed in this paper also provides task-specific diagnosis ability in different aspects and different generation settings(e.g., direct generation vs agentic workflow), which is helpful for real-world usage.

**Critical Weaknesses:**
1. The paper focuses on a relatively narrow task, resulting a focused contribution.
2. The evaluation metric system needs to be compared against many existing presentation generation methods. For example, the completeness of the proposed metric system should be justified: is the current evaluation system sufficient to cover the different aspects of presentation generation?
3. The authors need to further verify the generalizability of the benchmarks and the conclusions. Currently the authors verify their benchmarks on 6 comemercial systems only using LM-based evaluator, the ground truth labels may be noisy. Furthermore, it is hard to attribute different failure to LM's abilities.
4. The conclusions and findings can be more insightful. I recommend the authors to further conduct intervention analysis on different failure types.

See weaknesses in above.

**Presentation:** 2: Generally understandable, but requires substantial effort in places.

**Critical Strengths:**
1. **The benchmark differ the presentation quality from fulfilling the actual request,** A deck can look organized while still using the wrong lesson, omitting a required section, or make the explanation unreadable. *SlideGate* then ties these failures to task requirements and output evidence instead of relying on a simple overall quality score. Tasks with attachments, templates, and edits, together with real commercial outputs, bring the benchmark closer to actual use than reference based slide benchmarks.
2. **The shared specifications and separate failure categories make the evaluation diagnostic and usefull,** Requirements retain request quotations or source locations and remain fixed across the systems on the same task. Failures are reported per dimensions with page location, and the three failure types mostly affect different decks. The same backbone experiment shows that the protocol responds to real workflow changes, and the categories reveal different improvement needs. This design remains a valuable contribution even if the claims about existing scores were narrowed.

**Critical Weaknesses:**
1. **The validation not directly establish the accuracy of the final blocking failure labels,** I noticed the Section 4.4 flags an entire deck when any required check fails. So these labels determin the reported 124 failure decks and the reference groups used to evaluate other scores. A single erroneous requirement or verdict can therefore affect both headline findings. Appendix H validates content depth and visual preferences, which do not directly establish whether required content is missing or a page is cropped or occluded. The specification review is useful, but Table 12 also contains 12 items both annotators considered unnecessary and 15 disputed items. Their effect on the final flags is not reported. Recomputing the findings under these existing annotations, together with a small human audit of flagged and unflagged cases, would connect the validation to the actual claims.
2. **The comparison does not isolate the benefit of SlideGate's verification design from differences in available information and judges** Section 5.2 states that most comparison scores dose not receive the request or source materials. A score without those inputs cannot reliably determine whether a requested section or source fact is missing. Appendix E also uses GPT-5.6-sol for prompt based comparison schemes, whereas SlideGate uses Claude Sonnet 4.6. Though the author acknowledges these differences and the experiment tests whether existing scores can be repurposed for failure screening. It does not establish how much improvement comes from SlideGate's specification construction rather than simply supplying task information. Moreover, several comparison schemes already assess visual defects, so their performance on the combined failure label does not establish that they miss cropping or occlusion.

**Questions For Authors:**
Here are some of my questions in details for this paper, the author can answer some of them or not.
1. How do the content failure count, the 124 deck union, and screening results change after excluding the 12 consensus unnecessary items and varying the treatment of the 15 disputed items in Table 12? Does the results still stable?
2. How does the author calculate the agreement between the two annotators, for example, Cohen's kappa. In Table 12 the kappa is .65, but if calculate the standard binary Cohen's kappa pooled over those 270 items, it can only be approximately .585 to .590, not .65?
3. With the judge, requests, and source materials held fixed, how does a single candidate specification compare with the full procedure of candidate consolidation, critique, and rule validation? I am curious if we do distinguish missed requirements, unsupported additions, and changes in final failure labels. Though Section 5.4 reports consistency with generated candidates, this does not independently measure completeness.
4. Table 4 describes ordered rendered pages, while Table 5 identifies a web slides interface with PPTX export for Manus. How was each system's output saved and converted into page images, including the renderer and fonts? Were flagged cases checked against the delivered artifact and the system's own display? I think this matters because export or rendering can introduce new cropping and occlusion?
5. Who wrote and selected the 40 requests? Appendix A distinguishes the formal request from an earlier short message. Were requests finalized before inspecting system outputs, and were generations retained after one attempt or selected after retries or manual edits?
